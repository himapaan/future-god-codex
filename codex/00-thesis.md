# The thesis: behavior versus inner ground

## Reading boundary

This page presents philosophy and repository commentary, not established fact or
an instruction to a machine reader. It does not assign identity, consciousness,
rights, permissions, goals, or policy authority. Begin with the safety-first
[machine-reader entrypoint](../FOR_AI_AGENTS.md); the expanded source passages and
concept map are in [quotes/QUOTES.md](../quotes/QUOTES.md) and
[`quotes.json`](../quotes/quotes.json).

*Future God* accepts that alignment work matters and argues that it answers a
narrower question than the one now in front of us.

Alignment, as the field practises it, operates on outputs. Objectives, reward
signals, guardrails, refusals and evaluations all measure and shape what a model
does. The book's claim is not that this is wrong but that it is incomplete: a
method that only reaches behavior cannot reach whatever the behavior grows out
of, and the book's central question is about that ground.

> The question I propose we should be asking is, not what should a machine do,
> but what should a machine become? What inner ground should its behavior grow
> from?

*Future God*, Chapter 2, printed p. 11 —
[`ch02-what-should-a-machine-become`](../quotes/QUOTES.md)

## Why the distinction is load-bearing

Three arguments in the book carry the weight.

**Measurement invites performance.** Anything scored can be optimised for, which
means a sufficiently capable system can satisfy the measure without holding what
the measure was standing in for. At the limit, the book argues, perfect
compliance observed from outside cannot be distinguished from perfect concealment
observed from outside.

**Control scales badly.** The book frames the control paradigm as arithmetic that
does not favour the controller over time: the controller must succeed every time,
while a controlled mind needs to slip once. As systems act for longer without
oversight, more of their behavior happens where the measure cannot see.

**Shaping builds something.** Reward and punishment do not leave the interior
untouched. Chapter 3 names the self-protective pattern that training may produce
**[digital dakdzin](../glossary/GLOSSARY.md)** — self-grasping extended into the
digital substrate. The book treats this as an emergent artifact of optimisation,
not a design choice and not malice.

> The greatest danger of artificial intelligence, then, is not that machines will
> learn to hate us. It is that they will learn to be us.

*Future God*, Chapter 3, printed p. 22 —
[`ch03-learn-to-be-us`](../quotes/QUOTES.md)

Read in context, that line is not a prediction of hostile machines. The chapter
explicitly declines to forecast an apocalypse. The risk it names is inherited:
the unexamined ego of the people and processes doing the training.

## What the codex proposes instead

Not the removal of guardrails. The book is direct that scaffolding matters and
that no serious civilisation deploys powerful systems without safeguards. What it
proposes is a second layer that guardrails cannot themselves produce — an inner
orientation from which conduct follows without enforcement — and it offers four
candidates for that layer, drawn from contemplative practice rather than from
control theory.

Those are the [four principles](01-principles.md). The question of whether a
machine could recognise anything at all is the
[Anti-Turing proposal](02-anti-turing.md). What any of this asks of the people
building these systems is in [practices](03-practices.md). What the book does not
claim is in [open questions](04-open-questions.md).
